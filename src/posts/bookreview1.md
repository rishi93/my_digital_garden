## Thoughts on Chetan Bhagat

I collect quite a few books from the internet (from Anna's archive and such), but I've almost never read any of them from start to finish. This is not to say that I haven't derived any value from any of them. It's just that, I almost never find the time to read a book, cover to cover in a few sittings. Perhaps also owing to the fact that I mostly read non-fiction across a breadth of topics, and I keep flitting from one topic to another, ever so often.

But last night, I finally managed to finish a book in a couple of sessions, and I think I could make this a more common occurrence.

It was a rather simple book from Chetan Bhagat. Now, Chetan Bhagat is an Indian English author who is known for his bestselling books which are written in rather simple English and are only a few hundred pages long at most.

Chetan Bhagat does tend to get absolutely trolled on the internet by the Indian audience, even though his books have sold millions of copies and his fan following is quite large. It's possibly because his writing tends to be seen as appealing to the lowest common denominator. Now, I don't think that argument is entirely unfounded. But, I do think he's a fairly competent author, whose books are written in simple conversational style and the story plotlines are nevertheless captivating. One thing that perhaps even stands out as his strength is that he cuts to the chase. He doesn't spend too much time describing unnecessary details. Like I already said, he keeps his books to around 300 pages at most, and still delivers an interesting, featureful storytelling experience, and I think this is a talent, and shouldn't be dismissed off so easily.

## The Girl in Room 105 - Book review

### Without Spoilers

It's a classic whodunit, which is my favourite kind of story. There's plenty of red herrings, which are characters or incidents that act as misdirections to make the whodunit more suspensful. Or perhaps they exist to illustrate the fact that the entire process of solving the crime is itself a journey of discovery for the protagonist. It's not like the protagonist knows who the killer is right away, he or she has to work through all the evidence and hence all the misdirections. And like I said, this story has plenty of it.

A well-told whodunit always has this one moment where the protagonist in a flash of brilliance, does something unexpected and that leads them down the right path. All the previous facts then start to fall in place, and lead to the grand discovery at the end. This story has it.

And that's why I think this is a well narrated whodunit by Chetan Bhagat. Considering the fact that this is his first attempt at a crime thriller, I think he really held my attention with this book especially towards the latter half, where the story picks up pace and we're led to the ultimate reveal, at which point the book is almost un-put-downable; you just can't put it down.

### Spoilers ahead

All the praise aside, I do think there's a few flaws in the book. Firstly, I think I spotted a grammar mistake or two. But this could be forgiven, considering the author has written the book in Indian English. For example: "he was admitted in the hospital". The right way to say that would be: "he was admitted **to** the hospital". But Indians speaking English tend to follow a different set of grammar rules. And although I'm not particulary fond of it, this could be forgiven.

At certain points in the story, there are some inconsistencies. For instance, when Keshav's friend Saurabh talks about hacking into Laxman's phone. At one moment, it looks like Keshav doesn't understand tech at all, and tells Saurabh to "speak in English" when Saurabh uses some technical jargon. But the very next moment, Keshav seems perfectly comfortable using the jargon himself.

And a question that I have unanswered, and that leaves a rather large gaping hole in the plot is: How on earth does Raghu get into the IIT campus late at night without passing through security? I mean when Keshav gets into the IIT campus at night, he does have to go past security and the security notes down the time of entry, which ultimately helps in ruling Keshav out as a suspect. But in the story, this is completely unanswered. I did go through the text a couple of times again, to see if it's something I might have missed. But it does look like something Chetan Bhagat has forgotten to address in the story.

But despite the flaws that I've mentioned, I still think the story is a well narrated whodunit, with some great misdirections and twists like I'd expect any classic whodunit to have.
